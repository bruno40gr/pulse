## Press Release

### FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE

**CharterFlow Lets Enrichment Providers Say Yes to Every Funded Student — Without Hiring a Bureaucracy Department**

*One platform turns every charter school and SDP funding program into saved knowledge instead of staff memory, so providers onboard funded students in minutes, invoice correctly the first time, and know exactly when they'll get paid.*

ROCKLIN, CALIF. — [Launch date] — Today Layered Labs launched CharterFlow, a platform for California enrichment and therapy providers — music schools, tutors, therapists — who serve students funded through charter school instructional funds (Alta, South Sutter, Visions) and the Self-Determination Program via FMS providers (Mains'l, ACE FMS, Aveanna).

CharterFlow doesn't standardize funding organizations. It standardizes how vendors work with them. Alta stays Alta. Mains'l stays Mains'l. Their processes remain different — but inside CharterFlow those differences become data: which documents each one requires, which alternatives they accept, what must appear on every invoice, where it gets sent, who has to approve it, and when the money actually arrives. The vendor experiences one consistent workflow. The system handles which rules apply.

We call these organizations **payers**, the same term used across healthcare billing for any third party that authorizes or funds a service on someone else's behalf, because that's exactly what a charter program or an SDP financial management service is: never the family, never the provider, always the one on the other end of the paperwork.

The problem is real and expensive. Today, a provider's knowledge of how each funding program works lives in whichever staff member learned it the hard way. A front-desk employee doesn't know invoices need a state service code until a parent relays the rejection. A vendor application bounces three times because the acceptable documents were revealed one rejection email at a time. An invoice sits unpaid for weeks and nobody knows whether the delay is normal, stuck, or lost. The rational response for many small providers is the quiet one: when a family's funding source looks like too much hassle, decline the student.

CharterFlow's founding team ran that exact business. "We were that provider," said Bruno Wong Marchena, co-owner of Headliner Music Academy and founder of Layered Labs. "We had students on the autism spectrum reaching us through three different funding systems at once — and every one of those systems had to be survived separately before a single lesson was covered. We caught ourselves treating certain funding sources as too troublesome to accept. That's the moment we understood the real cost of this problem: it isn't paperwork hours. It's families who get a no because their money arrives with friction attached."

With CharterFlow, a provider's compliance documents are entered once and reused across every program. Invoices generate pre-formatted with the correct service codes, authorization numbers, and aggregation each funder requires, and route automatically — to the funder's inbox, their portal, or through the family, whichever that funder's process demands. A live dashboard shows every case by what it needs: blocked, on track, overdue, or rejected — with the reason and the owner. Families never create accounts or learn software; when a funder requires their signature or approval, they get one link and one tap.

## FAQ — Customers (vendors)

**What's actually in it for me?**
Four things, in order of how fast you'll feel them. First: you stop re-entering the same business information — the same EIN, address, and banking details currently appear three times in a single FMS enrollment packet; in CharterFlow they're entered once, ever. Second: invoices go out right the first time — correct service code, correct format, correct recipient — so they stop bouncing for reasons your staff had no way to know. Third: you can see your funded book in one glance — what's blocked and whose move it is, what's overdue against that funder's *actual* payment behavior, what got rejected and why. Fourth, and the one that changes your business: the next funded student who walks in with a program you've never heard of is an onboarding flow, not a judgment call about whether the hassle is worth it.

**Why wouldn't I just use spreadsheets? That's what I do now.**
A spreadsheet can track statuses. It cannot *know things*. It doesn't know Mains'l pays invoices under $500 the following Friday but holds invoices over $500 for three weeks — so it can't tell you that billing that student weekly instead of monthly gets you paid two weeks faster. It doesn't know ACE FMS rejects state tax documents but accepts a license application receipt. It doesn't generate the invoice, fill the enrollment packet, route the envelope to the family when that's what the funder requires, or notice that a payment is late *relative to that specific funder's real track record*. A spreadsheet also resets to zero when the person maintaining it leaves. CharterFlow is the difference between a list of what happened and a system that knows what to do.

**Isn't this still entirely manual? Someone has to set all this up.**
The honest answer: there is one-time work, and there is recurring work, and CharterFlow is built to make the first small and the second nearly zero. One-time: confirming a funding organization's profile (AI drafts it from the funder's own published packet; you review a short list where most fields are already verified) and mapping your billing export once. Recurring — the work you do every single week today — is what disappears: no retyping vendor info, no remembering codes, no rebuilding invoices per funder's format, no digging through email to figure out what's stuck. And the one-time work compounds: a profile confirmed once serves every student, every invoice, every future vendor on that funder. The manual work you do today is rework, forever. The manual work in CharterFlow is investment, once.

**My funders will never adopt new software. Doesn't this fail without them?**
They don't have to adopt anything, and that's deliberate. Funders keep receiving submissions exactly the way they do today — email, their own portal, or through the family. CharterFlow works entirely from the vendor's side. No logins, no behavior change, no permission required from anyone you don't control.

**What if my payer isn't in CharterFlow yet?**
That's the Discovery flow, not a dead end: upload whatever the payer published, the AI drafts the profile, you confirm it — and from then on that payer is covered for you and every provider after you. Early customers' payers get built first.

## FAQ — Families (asked by vendors on their behalf)

**What changes for the parents?**
They stop being the messaging layer of a bureaucracy. Today a parent relays invoice requirements from the FMS to the vendor (and loses precision in transit), forwards documents, chases approvals, and re-answers the same questions per provider. With CharterFlow, when a funder genuinely requires the family's action — an approval tap, a signature, adding the vendor in their portal — they receive one link with one specific action, pre-filled with everything already known. No account, no software, no becoming an expert in how Alta differs from Aveanna. Families whose children need these services most are the ones navigating the most systems at once; CharterFlow's explicit design goal is that a family's access to a provider should never depend on how many bureaucracies they can personally survive.

## FAQ — Internal / Investors

**Who is the customer, and why do they pay?**
The vendor — self-serve, acute weekly pain, revenue directly at stake (delayed cash, bounced invoices, declined students). Families benefit but never pay. Funding organizations are non-users at launch and the distribution channel later: every vendor on CharterFlow submits cleaner, more complete packets, which funders notice without being sold anything.

**Where's the network effect, concretely?**
The unit of compounding is the funding organization profile. Vendor #1 pays the cost of encoding Alta, Mains'l, ACE FMS, Aveanna. Vendor #2 arrives to find most of their funders already encoded — and their corrections improve the profiles for vendor #3. Every rejection, every observed payment date, every discovered document alternative makes the shared knowledge richer than any single vendor could maintain. The two metrics that prove it's working: coverage (what share of a new vendor's funders are already profiled at signup — must rise) and time-to-onboard a new funding organization (first profile: hours; fiftieth: minutes — must fall). If either goes flat, this is consulting with software on top, and we'll know early.

**Why hasn't this been built?**
The charter side has procurement software — for the *school's* internal purchasing, the wrong side of the transaction. The SDP side has nothing vertical at all. Each funding organization is individually too small for a statewide-ESA-platform player to chase, and no authorizer forces consolidation, so demand never aggregated. The market is underserved because it's fragmented — and we're explicit internally that fragmentation explains the opportunity, not the moat. The moat is the compounding profile library plus switching costs, both of which only exist after volume.

**Aren't you just maintaining an unmaintainable pile of bureaucratic variants?**
No — that failure mode is designed against with one rule: if a difference affects every vendor using an organization, it's profile data; if it affects one family or one conversation, it's a case note. Representatives are contacts, never business logic. The engine — one fixed pipeline of states every case moves through — never changes; only the rules within it do. And before serious code is written, Phase 0 validates the architecture empirically: five real organizations mapped side by side against one schema, with a pass/fail question — can ~90% of their differences be expressed as data? Two stress tests have already passed (a family-routed submission flow and a Regional Center delay both absorbed as data, not schema changes).

**What's the endgame with the FMS providers and charters themselves?**
Three stages, only the first assumed. Now: they're recipients — they get cleaner submissions and notice their vendor back-and-forth dropping. Next: recommenders — when a vendor's packet arrives complete and correctly formatted every time, the funder's cheapest support answer becomes "are you on CharterFlow?" Eventually: participants — funders maintaining their own profiles directly, updating a rule once and syncing every vendor instantly, which shifts maintenance to the source of truth and makes CharterFlow the operational layer between California's funded families and everyone who serves them. We are not pricing stage three into stage one — but the architecture (versioned profiles, per-field provenance) is built so it requires no rewrite.

**What's the strongest claim you can honestly make about access for families in need — and what claim are you *not* making?**
What we can prove from our own operating records: the same autistic child's access to one music school can require surviving three unrelated bureaucracies; a vendor approval stalled for weeks over a business-license technicality while a family's invoices sat unpayable; and our own academy treated some funding sources as too troublesome to accept — meaning families were getting nos because of process, not capacity. What we are *not* yet claiming: industry-wide statistics on how often providers decline funded families. That's a Phase 0 interview question ("have you ever declined a student because of their funding source?"), and it graduates into our materials only when the data exists. The thesis stands on the verified version alone: every point of vendor friction removed is a provider more able to say yes — and the families this affects most are the ones with the least slack to absorb a no.

**Biggest risks?**
(1) The abstraction fails Phase 0 validation — differences turn out to be structural, not data; the fallback is a visibility-first product, a smaller but real business. (2) Compounding stalls — coverage and onboarding-time metrics go flat; we'd be a services firm and would stop selling until fixed. (3) Scope regression to charter-only — it happened repeatedly in our own drafting; SDP/FMS in scope is a standing, explicit decision. (4) Profile staleness — mitigated by per-field confidence, verified-by metadata, versioning, and vendor flag-on-failure loops, but a wrong config that fails silently remains the sharpest product risk we carry.
